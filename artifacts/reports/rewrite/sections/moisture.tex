\section{Wet and dry soil conditions}
\label{sec:moisture}

We used weekly SMAP L4 surface soil moisture to describe departures from
usual seasonal conditions during April--July 2019 and June--August 2022,
the two event windows selected for the flood and drought case studies.
The analysis covered cells eligible for the crop-history analysis and
grouped them by state and event-year CDL label. The crop strata were corn
(1), soybean (5), winter wheat/soybean double crop (26), and oats (28).
SMAP's native 9~km resolution sets the spatial support of the moisture
information; assigning it to crop-grid cells does not provide independent
moisture measurements for individual fields.

For each cell and ISO week, we estimated a reference mean and sample
standard deviation from 2015--2021, giving at most seven annual
observations. We expressed each event observation as a departure from that
mean in standard-deviation units, with stored $z$-scores clipped to
$[-5,5]$. The reference includes 2019 but precedes 2022. These are therefore
retrospective descriptions against a common reference, with different
relationships to the two event years. Including 2019 can reduce its own
departure from the reference; the comparison does not constitute symmetric
held-out validation.

We also fitted a Normal--Inverse-Gamma model to account for uncertainty
in the reference mean and variance. Its conjugate update gives a
Student-$t$ posterior predictive distribution \citep{murphy2007conjugate}.
The prior mean was the regional mean for the corresponding week; the
variance prior used the regional median of the cell-level baseline
variances, with $\lambda_0=1$ and $\alpha_0=2$. We evaluated the predictive
cumulative distribution at each observed moisture value. This moisture
percentile approaches zero for unusually dry observations and one for
unusually wet observations. It is not the probability that drought is
present. The predictive scale combines moisture variability with parameter
uncertainty; it is neither a pure measure of epistemic uncertainty nor the
Student-$t$ standard deviation.

Figure~\ref{fig:moisture} compares mean $z$-scores for corn and soybean
across the two windows. State--crop means average the available
pixel-weeks, and threshold fractions use the same pixel-week denominator.
A cell observed in several weeks contributes several times, so these
fractions describe the combined spatial and temporal extent of unusual
conditions, rather than the share of fields affected.

The 2019 means were positive for corn and soybean in every study state.
South Dakota corn had a mean $z$-score of 1.16. In Iowa, 17.75\% of
2,901,150 corn pixel-weeks exceeded $z=1.5$, indicating repeated or
widespread departures above the weekly reference. This threshold measures
relative wetness; it does not establish soil saturation or field flooding.

The 2022 window showed a different pattern. Nebraska soybean had a mean
$z$-score of $-1.05$, and 40.54\% of its 947,648 pixel-weeks fell below
the tenth predictive percentile. North Dakota corn remained slightly
above its reference mean ($z=0.19$), showing that the dry signal was not
uniform across the study area. Kentucky's winter wheat/soybean double-crop
stratum had 41.96\% of 49,543 pixel-weeks below the tenth percentile.
That result concerns code~26 land cover and cannot be read as evidence
about winter wheat alone. The crop groups also occupy different locations,
so these contrasts do not isolate crop-specific responses to water stress.

These summaries identify where and when surface moisture was unusual
within the sampled crop-history population. Read alongside seasonal NDVI,
they provide context for crop-condition monitoring. The present analysis
does not test whether moisture anomalies precede vegetation changes or
predict yield loss. Independent event labels would also be needed to
measure detection accuracy or determine whether the Bayesian scoring
reduces false positives.
